# Corrosion pits on cable strands: measurement and assessment

Inspection office background note IO-B.5.

Corrosion of the galvanised steel wires is the main long-term threat to a stay cable. Water that reaches the strand through damaged sheathing or leaking anchorage seals removes the zinc coating and then attacks the steel. The attack is rarely uniform: it forms pits, and the deepest pit controls the loss of cross-section and the risk of a fatigue crack starting at the pit.

## How pits are measured

During a measurement campaign the inspector opens the sheathing at selected windows, cleans the strand and measures the pits with a depth gauge or a laser profilometer. The record keeps the depth of the deepest measured corrosion pit on the strand in mm. Shallow general corrosion of the zinc layer is noted separately and is not counted as a pit.

## What drives corrosion

* Moisture: relative humidity inside the cable above roughly 60% allows corrosion to proceed; below about 40% it practically stops.
* Temperature: higher temperatures speed up the reactions while the strand is wet.
* Contaminants: de-icing salts and airborne dust that holds moisture on the surface accelerate pitting.
* Time: corrosion damage accumulates over the service life and with the time since the last maintenance.

## Assessment

The inspector describes the corrosion condition of each window in words (no corrosion, zinc loss, light pitting, heavy pitting, broken wires) and photographs it. Pit depth is an input to the remaining-strength assessment carried out by the bridge engineer. Ambient readings such as temperature, humidity and dust are recorded with each campaign to explain the corrosion trend over time.
